HASA: Human-Aware Step Allocation for Efficient Video Diffusion Sampling

TL;DR HASA spends more denoising steps (NFEs) on human tokens and fewer on the background.

Text-to-video diffusion transformers can now render convincing people, but every clip costs dozens of full forward passes over tens of thousands of spatiotemporal tokens, and the standard sampler spends the same number of denoising steps on a blurry background as on a speaking face or a gesturing hand. We present Human-Aware Step Allocation (HASA), a heterogeneous step-allocation sampler that gives every token its own step budget and spends the budget where that matters most, without retraining the generator. Early in sampling, HASA maps the model's own clean-video prediction to low-resolution images with a light-weight latent adapter, detects human keypoints on them with a frozen pose estimator, and routes face, hand and body tokens into a high-budget group that is updated at every step, while the remaining tokens are updated only every k-th step. Skipped tokens stay in the attention context through a synchronized key-value cache and are advanced with their cached velocity. Because the clean-video prediction sharpens as denoising proceeds, the detections are refreshed twice during sampling. We evaluate HASA on a new benchmark of 100 human-centric prompts (face close-ups, upper-body gestures, full-body actions, multi-person interactions and daily activities) on four generators, from 1.3B to 14B parameters and including a joint audio-video model, at matched compute budget. Quality and prompt alignment are measured by a pairwise vision-language judge against each model's full-step output, calibrated with a different-seed full-step sample. At 25% compute, HASA improves over uniform step reduction on three of the four models (up to +14.1 points, p<0.01) and reaches an operating point where the existing caching methods break down. The human prior adds a consistent gain over random token groups on the models that benefit from heterogeneous allocation, largest for full-body motion. We also report where HASA does not help: at 50% compute on the two largest models, where simply halving the step count is already as good as the full-step reference.

Heterogeneous Step Allocation. Tokens are split into groups with different step budgets; only the active tokens run through the network, attend to the full (fresh + cached) keys and values, and every token is advanced with its latest velocity.

How the Q and A scores are computed

Each score compares one video with its model's full-step reference from the same seed. A vision-language judge (Qwen3.8-27B) sees 16 frames of both videos and answers a single letter, A or B, for the video it prefers. Instead of keeping only the letter, we read the probabilities the judge assigns to the two answer tokens and normalize them: P(this video is better) = pA / (pA + pB). A confident judgment gives a value near 0 or 1, an uncertain one a value near 0.5.

  • Q (quality): which video has better technical quality: sharpness and texture, noise, artifacts, well-formed faces, hands and bodies, temporal stability and natural motion. Differences in content are ignored.
  • A (alignment): which video matches the prompt better: the people and their appearance, actions, gestures and expressions, the setting and the camera.

Every video is judged four times: with the video shown first and shown second (to cancel the judge's position bias), each at two offsets of the sampled frames. The score is the mean of the four probabilities, in percent. For example, Q 73.7 averages 0.71, 0.73, 0.76 and 0.76: the judge consistently leans toward this video. A score of 50 means the video is as good as the reference; a full-step video with a different seed, equally good but different, averages 47–51 on our four models, which is the practical ceiling for any accelerated sampler.
